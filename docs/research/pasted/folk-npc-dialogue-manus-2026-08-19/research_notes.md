# Research Notes — SynapticGM Folk Voice Expectations

**Research date:** 2026-08-19 (GMT+1)

## Source 1 — World History Encyclopedia: *Elves & Dwarves in Norse Mythology*

**URL:** <https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1695/elves--dwarves-in-norse-mythology/>  
**Accessed:** 2026-08-19

### Verifiable findings

1. The entry describes Old Norse *álfar* and *dvergar* as minor divine figures and notes shared motifs of creating precious objects, skill, agility, and **moral ambiguity**.
2. It connects some dwarves to rocks/caves and to the making of valuable objects, but it also documents ambiguous or overlapping classifications among dwarves, dark-elves, and light-elves.
3. It states that very little is known of elves in Norse myth; it mentions an elven sacrifice that may relate to harvest and ancestor worship.
4. It documents the figure of Völund/Wayland as a renowned smith, including a violent revenge story. This supports neither blanket “noble elf” nor blanket “cheerful dwarf” portrayals.
5. It notes later or overlapping negative traditions in which elves could be associated with illness; it quotes older terminology connected with “elf-shot” and nightmares.

### Design consequence

Use **craft, secrecy, reciprocity, danger, and ambiguity** as optional heritage motifs—not as biological speech or moral rules. Do not claim a single pan-European “elf” or “dwarf” personality. All cadence and social-default entries in the package are explicitly **SPECULATIVE game-design conventions**, except where a citation identifies a specific documented motif.

### Citation key

[1] Manea, Irina-Maria. “Elves & Dwarves in Norse Mythology.” *World History Encyclopedia*, 8 March 2021. Accessed 2026-08-19. <https://www.worldhistory.org/article/1695/elves--dwarves-in-norse-mythology/>

## Initial IP and sensitivity boundary

The requested guide will treat names such as **Elf, Dwarf, Goblin, Troll, Vampire, Ghost/spirit, and Merfolk** as broad public-folklore umbrellas, while refusing franchise-specific histories, dialects, locations, slogans, items, or invented languages. **Orc, smallfolk, beastfolk, dragonfolk, and all original entries** will be handled as SynapticGM-facing game labels with original portrayal guidance, never as claims about a unitary real-world tradition. The package will not reproduce licensed character voices or product terminology.

## Evidence policy for final deliverables

- **FOLKLORE-CITED**: a narrowly stated motif tied to a listed source.
- **SPECULATIVE**: an original, optional play heuristic for familiar fantasy expectations.
- **CONTRACTUAL**: SynapticGM product-law behavior. This is not a folklore claim.
- **INDIVIDUAL OVERRIDE**: named NPC or CampaignContract instruction that outranks folk defaults.

No source will be used to justify real-world ethnic, regional, disability, or language stereotypes.

## Source 2 — Encyclopedia.com: *Trolls*

**URL:** <https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/trolls>  
**Accessed:** 2026-08-19

### Verifiable findings

1. The entry identifies the relevant tradition as Norse/Scandinavian and describes early trolls variously as giants, mystical nature beings, or distant relatives of gods.
2. It records motifs of night roaming, stone transformation in sunlight, and links to rocks, waterfalls, and landscapes.
3. It states that later Christianized stories often portrayed trolls as wicked and introduced prayer as a weapon. This illustrates historical change rather than a universal creature property.
4. It documents a recurring human–troll **bargain** motif: people may have to outwit the troll; trolls are characterized as clever or crafty tricksters who can sometimes help humans but often create problems.

### Design consequence

A troll NPC may ask exacting questions, protect a boundary, or test a bargain’s wording. It must **not** be made uniformly stupid, uniformly violent, or universally vulnerable to any one device. The final package treats bargain literalism and boundary concern as a cited optional motif; voice cadence is original game-design guidance.

### Citation key

[2] “Trolls.” *Encyclopedia.com* (Cengage reference entry). Accessed 2026-08-19. <https://www.encyclopedia.com/history/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/trolls>


## Source 3 — The Angry Noodle: *Creating a Fictional Culture for Your World*

**URL:** <https://theangrynoodle.com/creating-fictional-cultures-and-races-for-your-world-while-avoiding-stereotypes-and-caricatures/>  
**Accessed:** 2026-08-19

### Verifiable findings

1. The essay states that culture and race are not the same, and explicitly cautions against assuming that people will behave or live a particular way solely because of “race.”
2. Its visible section structure includes guidance against making a group’s main trait evil/violence/greed/selfishness and against “exaggerated accents or dialogue for joke characters.”
3. This is an authorial design essay, not a scholarly folklore source. It is used as an aligned craft perspective, not as evidence that folklore works in one way.

### Design consequence

The package hard-codes **voice as an optional social register, never a competence, morality, or intelligence statistic**. Each folk has three override examples, named-NPC memory has priority, and accented spellings are prohibited. “All members of a folk are [moral trait]” cannot pass the evaluation suite.

### Citation key

[3] The Angry Noodle. “Creating a Fictional Culture for Your World.” 10 June 2021. Accessed 2026-08-19. <https://theangrynoodle.com/creating-fictional-cultures-and-races-for-your-world-while-avoiding-stereotypes-and-caricatures/>

The browser page exposed extensive regional/topic links (including India, Babylonia, Greece, Hungary, Bohemia, Moravia, Silesia, Russia, Ukraine, Bulgaria, Serbia, and Transylvania), supporting the preliminary conclusion that “vampire” is not a single undifferentiated tradition. The final vampire material will therefore label the invitation/threshold protocol **SPECULATIVE / modern-gothic interface convention**, not a universal or historical folklore rule. This distinction is non-negotiable even if an NPC uses the convention in a given campaign.


## Source 4 — Encyclopedia.com: *Vampire*

**URL:** <https://www.encyclopedia.com/literature-and-arts/classical-literature-mythology-and-folklore/folklore-and-mythology/vampires>  
**Accessed:** 2026-08-19

### Verifiable findings

1. The entry defines a folklore vampire as a dead person returning from the grave in spirit form to feed on the living, or as a living sorcerer taking a destructive form; it gives a broad, historically varied geography including ancient India, Babylonia, Greece, and several Slavic/Balkan and central/eastern European traditions.
2. Its account shows that vampire features and prevention practices vary by location, including different proposed origins and anti-vampire measures such as beheading, removing the heart, impaling, or burning.
3. The readable entry does **not** establish a universal invitation/threshold prohibition. It therefore cannot support the claim that “vampires need an invitation” is a universal folklore rule.

### Design consequence

SynapticGM treats **threshold / invitation etiquette** as an opt-in, campaign-defined vampire protocol inspired by later Gothic expectation, explicitly marked **SPECULATIVE**. A vampire may observe it because this NPC, clan, jurisdiction, or CampaignContract does—not because every vampire does.

## Source 5 — Encyclopedia.com: *Mermaid* and *Mermaids and Mermen*

**URLs:**
- <https://www.encyclopedia.com/literature-and-arts/classical-literature-mythology-and-folklore/folklore-and-mythology/mermaids>
- <https://www.encyclopedia.com/science/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/mermaids-and-mermen>

**Accessed:** 2026-08-19

### Verifiable findings

1. The entries describe broad variation across traditions, including water-lady / sea-being motifs, and explicitly distinguish early bird-bodied Greek sirens from later fish-tailed mermaid imagery.
2. The sources document European sea-folklore motifs of music, storms, danger to sailors, riddles, warning appearances, and occasional human–merperson marriage narratives. They do **not** establish a universal seductress or villain archetype.
3. The entries include later literary adaptations and gendered symbolism. These materials are unsuitable grounds for sexualizing a folk, particularly in Kid Mode.

### Design consequence

Merfolk defaults may use water, tides, currents, and navigation as optional metaphors; they may prefer negotiated safety and direct warning at sea. They must not default to seduction, singing, or female-coded presentation. In Kid Mode, exclude predatory/sexual material and turn any “song” motif into practical call-and-response, weather knowledge, or a non-coercive work chant.
